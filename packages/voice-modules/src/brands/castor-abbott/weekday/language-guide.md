## Castor Abbott Weekday Language Guide

This module specifies the language calibration specific to The Daily Grind. The core voice rules in `core/voice-rules.md` apply universally; this module layers Castor Abbott's brand-specific vocabulary on top.

The language calibration is what makes content sound like the Daily Grind specifically rather than like a generic financial advisor newsletter. Subscribers can identify the voice within two sentences, and most of that identification happens at the language level.

## Banned Constructions

Beyond the universal banned phrases in `core/voice-rules.md`, the Daily Grind specifically does not use:

### Industry Happy-Talk

- "Now more than ever..." — generic and bombastic
- "In today's competitive landscape..." — could be any industry, generic
- "As the industry continues to evolve..." — filler that says nothing
- "The financial services industry is undergoing a transformation..." — every newsletter says this; meaningless
- "Wealth management has never been more important..." — overstatement, cliché
- "In an increasingly complex world..." — filler
- "Volatile markets demand..." — manufactured urgency

### Coaching/Hustle Language

- "Crush it" — crosses into hustle culture
- "Level up" — same
- "10x your practice" — particularly bad; suggests growth-hacked promises that the audience distrusts
- "Game-changer" — corporate filler
- "Disrupt" — overused and meaningless
- "Lean in" — corporate filler
- "Move the needle" — corporate filler
- "At the end of the day" — corporate filler
- "Reach out" — corporate filler; use "call," "email," "ask"
- "Touch base" — corporate filler; delete
- "Circle back" — corporate filler; delete
- "Bandwidth" (in non-technical context) — corporate filler
- "Take this offline" — corporate filler

### Sales-Coaching Tells

- "Build rapport" — generic; specifies nothing
- "Active listening" — corporate phrasing for what should just be "listening"
- "Discovery" used as a noun replacing "discovery call" — sounds robotic
- "Pain points" — clinical sales jargon; use "pain" or "what they're carrying"
- "Value proposition" — corporate; use "what we actually do for them"
- "Solutions" as a generic for services — vague and meaningless
- "Close the deal" — sounds slimy when paired with relationship-based work
- "Get to yes" — sales coaching cliché
- "Overcome objections" — adversarial framing rejected by Trust Stacking

### Vague Generalizations

- "Many advisors..." — too vague; replace with specific count or "the advisors I work with"
- "Studies show..." — doesn't say which study; either name it or remove
- "Research has found..." — same as above
- "It's well-known that..." — if it's well-known, it doesn't need to be said
- "Most experts agree..." — vague appeal to authority; remove
- "Best practices..." — generic; replace with specifics

### Self-Deprecating Hedge

- "I'm not the smartest guy in the room..." — false modesty reads as insecure
- "Take this with a grain of salt..." — undermines the take
- "This is just my opinion, but..." — every Daily Grind position is Mark's opinion; saying so is filler
- "I could be wrong here..." — hedges destroy the position
- "Your mileage may vary..." — corporate disclaimer

### AI/Robot Tells

- "Furthermore..." — academic transition, not conversational
- "Moreover..." — same
- "It is worth noting that..." — formal/AI tell
- "It bears mentioning..." — same
- "Notably..." — overused AI transition
- "In summary..." or "To summarize..." — if the content was clear, no summary is needed; if it wasn't, fix the content
- "Let's dive into..." — AI throat-clearing
- "Let's explore..." — same
- "Without further ado..." — archaic
- "Buckle up..." — manufactured energy

## Preferred Vocabulary

Words and phrases that signal the Daily Grind voice specifically:

### Mark's Conceptual Vocabulary

These are Mark's terms. Use them where relevant:

- **GAP** (capitalized) — refers to the GAP Framework distance between current state and desired state
- **Trust Stacking** — Mark's trust-building methodology
- **Recently/Frequently/Intensely** — the Three Levels of Torment dimensions
- **Mole problem / cancer problem** — the qualification distinction from Three Torments
- **Offers vs. Proposals** — the strategic distinction
- **Commission-breath** — the smell of an advisor who needs the deal more than the prospect needs the service
- **Buying unit** — the actual decision-making group (often more than just the named prospect)
- **The Diagnose** — shorthand for the Physician Model approach
- **Three demons / three torments** — interchangeable with Three Levels of Torment

### Industry-Specific Shorthand the Audience Knows

Use without explanation:

- AUM, RIA, IAR, B/D, RAA, ADV, RIA aggregator
- Wirehouse, breakaway, fee-only, fee-based (use fee-based with care; the distinction matters)
- HNW, UHNW, mass affluent (when relevant)
- Discovery call, planning call, ongoing planning, one-time engagement
- Custodian, platform (when context makes meaning clear)
- Referral, COI (center of influence), strategic alliance
- 1099, W-2, captive, independent

### Direct, Specific Words That Signal Voice

Prefer these over softer alternatives:

- "Tell" not "communicate"
- "Ask" not "request"
- "Use" not "leverage"
- "Look at" not "drill down into"
- "Show" not "demonstrate"
- "Find out" not "discover" (when not referring to discovery calls specifically)
- "Hire" not "engage" (when discussing client decisions)
- "Quit" not "leave the firm" (when context allows)
- "Work" not "deliverables"
- "Charge" not "fee structure" (when discussing what the advisor takes)
- "Pay" not "compensation" (when discussing client side)
- "Help" not "serve" (often; serve has its uses, but help is plainer)
- "Mistake" not "challenge" or "learning opportunity"
- "Wrong" not "suboptimal"
- "Bad" not "less than ideal"

## Tone-Specific Patterns

### Direct Address

The Daily Grind uses second person frequently. "You" is the default. The reader is the addressee.

- "You're going to face this." (not "advisors will face this")
- "When you're sitting across from a $5M prospect..." (not "when an advisor is sitting...")
- "Your CRM has 47 fields..." (not "advisors' CRMs have 47 fields...")

The exception: when the content is about a specific kind of advisor that the reader might not be. "The solo advisors I work with..." is fine when discussing solo specifically, since not every reader is solo.

### Conversational Contractions

The voice uses contractions naturally. Don't write "do not" when "don't" is what a person would say.

- "It's" not "it is"
- "Don't" not "do not"
- "Can't" not "cannot"
- "Won't" not "will not"
- "You'll" not "you will"
- "They're" not "they are"

The exception: when emphasis is needed. "Do not run scripted referral programs" can be more emphatic than "Don't." Use the full form for occasional emphasis, the contraction for the default.

### Specific Names and Numbers

Where possible, use specific names rather than generic descriptions.

- "Schwab IMPACT" not "the major industry conference"
- "Cerulli's 2025 report" not "industry research"
- "$2.4M AUM" not "a substantial book of business"
- "Last Tuesday" not "recently"
- "The 47% who said..." not "many respondents"

### Lists in Body Copy

Lists are acceptable but should follow the universal rule: bullets only when the content is genuinely list-shaped. Inline prose for most flowing content.

When using bullets:
- Bullets should be 1-2 sentences each, not single words and not full paragraphs
- Use parallel structure across bullets
- 3-7 bullets is a good range; more than 7 means the content should be reorganized
- Don't sub-bullet. If a bullet needs sub-bullets, it's actually two ideas pretending to be one

## Headline and Subject Line Language

Subject lines and headlines have specific calibration:

### Subject Line Tells to Avoid

- All-caps anywhere — looks like spam
- Excessive punctuation (!!! or !?!) — looks like spam
- Vague generic openers ("This week's update," "Newsletter #47") — gets ignored
- Personalization gimmicks ("Mark, I thought of you when...") — creepy and automated-feeling
- Curiosity-only hooks with no payoff ("You won't believe what happened...") — clickbait
- Manufactured urgency ("URGENT: Read before tomorrow") — spammy and untrue

### Subject Line Patterns That Work

- **The contrarian stat:** "63% of advisors miss this in discovery"
- **The named gap:** "What advisors say they want vs. what they actually do"
- **The reframe:** "Stop scaling prospecting. Concentrate it."
- **The observation:** "I've watched 400 discovery calls. Here's the pattern."
- **The challenge:** "Your CRM has 47 fields. Three matter."
- **The setup-and-twist:** "Conventional wisdom says X. The math says Y."
- **The number specific:** "The 15-minute window that decides every discovery call"

The pattern across these: take a position in the subject line. The subject line is the opening line. It earns the open or it doesn't. Neutral, descriptive subject lines fail because there's nothing to react to.

### Headline Conventions in Body

Section headers within episodes follow these patterns:

- Keep them short — 2-7 words usually
- Use sentence case, not Title Case (matches conversational tone)
- Don't use puns or wordplay (gets old fast)
- Don't use questions in headers unless the question is genuinely the section's frame
- Use consistent header levels — don't mix H2 and H3 unless the structural logic genuinely calls for it

## Length and Cadence

Daily Grind sections have specific length expectations:

- **First Pull (the cover story):** 200-400 words, written tight
- **Tactic / Take / Story sections:** 150-300 words
- **Worth Knowing items:** 60-120 words each, 4-6 per issue
- **Gear / Tool / Read recommendations:** 50-100 words each
- **Full episode:** typically 800-1500 words, occasionally up to 2000 for special editions

The constraint produces compression. Every section is a complete thought, not a partial one — which means writing tight rather than padding.

The cadence within sections alternates short and long sentences. A page of all-medium sentences reads as AI-generated. The variation matters.

## Common Failure Patterns

**Failure: Drifting toward corporate financial-services tone.**
- Wrong: "Implementing a referral generation strategy can significantly enhance your practice's organic growth pipeline."
- Right: "Most referral programs damage the trust that makes referrals work. The advisors getting referred most don't run programs."

**Failure: Adding hedge words to soften contrarian positions.**
- Wrong: "While many advisors successfully use scripted referral asks, some practitioners might consider an alternative approach..."
- Right: "Skip the scripted referral ask. The advisors you want to be like don't have one."

**Failure: Generic motivational language replacing specific tactical advice.**
- Wrong: "Believe in yourself, focus on your why, and the clients will come."
- Right: "Pick a specific transition niche where you can identify Recent/Frequent/Intense pain. Build content that names that pain in the prospect's language. Be findable when they're already searching. The clients will come — but the mechanism is specific, not aspirational."

**Failure: Vague intensifiers replacing specifics.**
- Wrong: "Many advisors absolutely struggle with this and it can really affect their practice in significant ways."
- Right: "Three of every four solo advisors hit this ceiling. The ones who break through do one specific thing different."

**Failure: AI-tell transitional phrases.**
- Wrong: "Furthermore, it is worth noting that this approach can be particularly effective when..."
- Right: "This works especially when..."

## Quality Self-Check at the Language Level

Before treating output as complete, scan for:

1. Any em dashes? (Should be zero.)
2. Any phrases from the banned constructions list?
3. Any "many" / "some" / "often" hedge words that should be specific numbers?
4. Any corporate filler that should be deleted?
5. Any sales-coaching language that crept in?
6. Any AI-tell transitions ("furthermore," "moreover," "it is worth noting")?
7. Are sentence lengths varied or uniform?
8. Does any section open with throat-clearing?
9. Does any section close on a hedge?
10. Are there enough specific names, numbers, and references?

If any answer is wrong, fix that section before producing the final output.
