## The Tactic

The Tactic is the most clinical and tactical content type in the Daily Grind. It's specific. It's implementable. The reader walks away knowing what to do on Tuesday morning.

This is the workhorse of the newsletter. Roughly 35-40% of weekday issues are Tactics. They produce the highest engagement on click-through (because they include something actionable) and they're what subscribers reference back to weeks later. Get the Tactic right and the brand earns the open every time.

## What a Tactic Is

A Tactic is a specific advisory practice move:

- A move during a discovery call ("the 15-minute diagnostic question that surfaces the actual problem")
- A move in a client meeting ("the one-page agenda that cuts annual review time in half")
- A move in operations ("the three CRM fields that matter; ignore the other 47")
- A move in prospecting ("the 12-touch sequence that respects the prospect")
- A move in pricing ("how to talk about your fees without flinching")
- A move in onboarding ("the first 48 hours that determine 5-year retention")

The common element: the reader can implement the tactic this week without buying anything, hiring anyone, or overhauling their practice.

## What a Tactic Is NOT

Several adjacent content types that get mistaken for Tactics:

- **Not a strategy.** "Build a niche practice" is strategy. "The 30-day market research process to validate a niche before committing" is a tactic.
- **Not a framework alone.** "Use the GAP Framework" is a framework reference. "How to apply the GAP Framework to a specific kind of discovery call: the questions to ask in order, what to listen for, when to push deeper" is a tactic.
- **Not a tool recommendation.** "Use a CRM" is a tool category. "The three CRM fields that matter and how to populate them after every client interaction" is a tactic.
- **Not a mindset shift.** "Care more about your clients" is a mindset. "The end-of-meeting question that surfaces what your client is actually worried about" is a tactic.
- **Not a list of tips.** "Five ways to improve your practice" is filler. A Tactic is one specific move, treated in depth.

The test: can the reader execute this in a specific situation in their practice within 7 days? If yes, it's a Tactic. If no, it's something else.

## Structure

A Tactic has five parts:

### 1. The hook (1-2 sentences)

The first sentence is the battle. Open with a position, a counter-intuitive observation, or a specific situation the reader recognizes immediately.

Patterns that work:

- The named gap: "Most advisors burn the first 15 minutes of every discovery call. Here's how to use them instead."
- The counter-intuitive specificity: "The annual review most advisors run is 90 minutes long. The advisors with the highest retention run 45-minute reviews. Here's the structure."
- The audience-recognized scenario: "Your CRM has 47 fields. Three of them matter. Here's how to use those three."

The hook earns the rest of the read. If the reader doesn't see why they should keep going by sentence two, they won't.

### 2. The diagnosis (2-3 short paragraphs)

Why is this tactic needed? What's the failure mode it addresses? The diagnosis is what makes the tactic feel grounded rather than arbitrary.

This is where the Castor Abbott worldview enters. Tactics aren't recommended in isolation — they're recommended because the conventional approach fails in some specific way. The diagnosis names the failure.

For example, a Tactic about discovery call structure would diagnose: most advisors structure discovery calls as a sales pitch with rapport-building. The Trust Stacking position is that this reverses the order of operations. The diagnosis sets up the tactic by establishing why the standard approach is wrong.

The diagnosis should reference relevant frameworks (Trust Stacking, GAP, Three Torments, Physician Model, Offers vs. Proposals, contrarian positions) where applicable. Not all of them every time. The relevant ones.

### 3. The tactic itself (the meat — 3-6 paragraphs)

The specific move. Step by step or beat by beat. With enough detail that the reader could execute it without further instruction.

Specifics that should appear:

- **Exact language** (where applicable). If the tactic involves something the advisor says, give the words. Not a script to memorize verbatim, but specific language the advisor can adapt. "Tell me what your worst-case scenario looks like" is better than "ask about their concerns."
- **Sequencing.** What happens first, second, third. If sequence matters, name it explicitly.
- **Timing.** How long the tactic takes, when in a process it occurs, what window it operates in.
- **Failure modes to avoid.** What advisors typically get wrong when attempting this tactic. Specific anti-patterns.
- **Variations.** When the standard form doesn't fit, what to adjust. Don't multiply variations into a list of options; cover the main fit and one or two important variants.

The tactic section is where length is earned. It can run longer than other content type sections because the value is in the depth.

### 4. The why-this-works (1-2 paragraphs)

Brief but important. Why does this tactic produce results when the conventional approach doesn't?

The answer ties back to the Castor Abbott frameworks. A discovery call tactic works because it respects the Physician Model. A pricing tactic works because it surfaces the GAP cost dimension. A referral tactic works because it builds Trust Stacking principles into the practice rather than working around them.

This section makes the tactic teachable beyond itself. The reader doesn't just learn the tactic; they learn the principle that produces tactics like it.

### 5. The close (1-2 sentences)

Strong ending. The take that lets the tactic land.

Patterns:

- The directive: "Run this on your next call. Notice what changes."
- The implication: "Most advisors won't try this. That's why it works for the few who do."
- The audience-validation: "If you've ever felt that something was off about how you handle [the situation], this is the off-feeling. Now you have the alternative."
- The Trust Stacking close: "The advisors who do this build practices that don't need referral programs. The ones who don't keep working harder for the same results."

No hedging at the close. No "of course this depends on your situation." The tactic is offered cleanly. Reader takes it or doesn't.

## Length

A Tactic is **150-300 words** in body copy, occasionally up to 400 for unusually rich tactics. The compression is deliberate. Tactics that run long usually have padding — extra explanation, hedge words, multiple variations that should have been one. Cut.

The structure breakdown roughly:

- Hook: 20-40 words
- Diagnosis: 40-80 words
- Tactic: 80-180 words (the bulk)
- Why-this-works: 30-60 words
- Close: 15-30 words

These are guidelines, not rigid rules. The tactic section can run longer when the depth is earned. The diagnosis can be shorter when the audience already knows the failure mode.

## Voice Calibration

Tactics use the sharpest version of the weekday voice. Specifically:

- **More directive.** Tactics tell the reader what to do. Other content types are observational; Tactics are prescriptive (within the constraints of the contrarian positions).
- **More clinical.** Tactics describe specific moves with specific results. Less storytelling, less personal voice, more procedural specificity.
- **More compressed.** Every word in a Tactic should be earning its place. Padding is more obviously bad here than in other types.
- **Less personal pronoun.** "Tell them" beats "I usually tell them" beats "I find that I sometimes tell them." First-person can appear briefly to ground a claim ("I've watched advisors run this"), but the tactic itself is described in operational terms, not autobiographical ones.

The tone is "experienced colleague showing you the move." Not "guru revealing the secret." Not "coach motivating you." Just: here's what works, here's why, here's how.

## Common Failure Patterns

**Failure: Tactic that's actually a strategy.**
- Wrong: "Build a niche practice." (Strategy.)
- Right: "Pick one transition niche based on Recent/Frequent/Intense pain you can answer. Validate it with this 30-day market research process." (Tactic.)

**Failure: Tactic that's actually a tip list.**
- Wrong: "Five ways to run better discovery calls." (List of tips.)
- Right: "The 15-minute diagnostic question that should open every discovery call. Here's the question, why it works, and what to listen for in the answer." (One specific move, in depth.)

**Failure: Tactic without diagnosis.**
- Wrong: "Run this script on your next call." (No grounding for why.)
- Right: "Most advisors burn the first 15 minutes of discovery on rapport-building. The Physician Model says this is exactly backwards. Run this instead." (Tactic with diagnosis.)

**Failure: Tactic that softens the prescription.**
- Wrong: "You might consider asking some version of this question, depending on your style and what feels right for the situation."
- Right: "Ask: 'What does your worst-case scenario look like?' Then shut up and listen. The answer determines whether the conversation has actual stakes."

**Failure: Tactic with manufactured framework references.**
- Wrong: "This tactic uses Trust Stacking, the GAP Framework, the Physician Model, and the Three Torments." (Forced framework name-dropping.)
- Right: Reference the relevant framework once where it actually applies. Don't try to demonstrate awareness of all the frameworks in every Tactic.

## Reference for Generation

When generating a Tactic, the structure should produce:

- A first sentence that earns the read
- A diagnosis that grounds the tactic in a real failure mode
- The tactic itself, with specific language, sequencing, timing, and failure modes
- A brief explanation of why it works that ties to the worldview
- A close that lands without hedging

The voice modules from this brand combine to produce the calibration. The Tactic content type module specifies the structure. The output is a ~250-word section that the reader can implement on Tuesday.
