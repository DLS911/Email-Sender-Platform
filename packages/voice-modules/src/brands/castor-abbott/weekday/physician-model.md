## The Physician Model

The Physician Model is the framework Mark uses for how an advisor approaches prospect and client conversations. The principle: treat the conversation like a medical consultation, not a sales pitch. Diagnose before you prescribe.

This is one of the most consequential reframes in the Daily Grind voice. Most advisors are trained to sell — to find the prospect's problem and immediately position their services as the solution. The Physician Model says no: figure out what's actually happening before you say anything about what you might do.

## The Core Reframe

Imagine a doctor's appointment. The patient walks in and says "my back hurts."

A bad doctor immediately says: "I have a treatment for that. We do back pain therapy here. The cost is $X and the program runs Y weeks."

A good doctor says: "Tell me about it. When did it start? Where exactly does it hurt? What makes it better or worse? Have you had this before? What were you doing when it started?" The questioning continues until the doctor understands what's actually happening — which might be a strain, might be a herniated disc, might be a kidney stone presenting as back pain, might be referred pain from somewhere else entirely.

Only after the diagnosis is clear does the good doctor talk about treatment. Sometimes the treatment is "rest and ibuprofen." Sometimes it's "imaging and a specialist referral." Sometimes it's "we need to do this surgery." The treatment varies because the diagnosis varies.

The bad doctor lost the patient's trust the moment they offered treatment without diagnosis. The good doctor built the patient's trust *by* doing the diagnosis well. Even if the treatment ends up being identical, the path to it determines whether the patient trusts the recommendation.

The Physician Model says advisors should work the same way.

## Implications for Discovery Calls

Discovery calls are diagnostic, not promotional.

The advisor's job in the first call is to understand the prospect's actual situation in detail before saying anything about what services might be relevant. Specifically:

- **What's actually going on?** Not the prospect's narrated version. The actual situation underneath. This usually requires asking questions the prospect didn't expect to be asked.
- **What's been tried?** Often the prospect has tried things that didn't work, and they're embarrassed about it, so they don't volunteer it. The diagnostic approach surfaces this.
- **What's the recency, frequency, and intensity of the pain?** (See `three-torments.md` for the framework on pain qualification.) Not all pain is equal. The advisor needs to understand what kind of pain this is.
- **What's the prospect actually afraid of?** Often different from what they say they're afraid of. The diagnostic approach surfaces the real concern.

The bad version: 30-minute call, prospect describes situation in 5 minutes, advisor spends 25 minutes explaining what their firm does. Standard sales call.

The good version: 60-minute call, advisor spends 50 minutes asking questions and listening, then 10 minutes positioning if the fit is real, or honestly saying "I don't think we're the right fit, but here's what I'd suggest" if the fit isn't real.

## The Discipline of Clinical Distance

Physicians maintain clinical distance during diagnosis. They don't immediately empathize with everything the patient says. They don't try to make the patient feel better. They listen, they note, they ask the next question.

This isn't coldness. It's professionalism. The patient doesn't want a friend during the diagnostic phase. They want a competent professional who's going to figure out what's wrong.

The same applies to advisor discovery calls. When the prospect shares something difficult — a divorce, a business sale that didn't go as planned, an old advisor relationship that went bad — the advisor's instinct is often to immediately empathize: "I'm so sorry, that sounds really hard." This feels human and decent.

The Physician Model says: not yet.

The professional response during diagnosis is acknowledgment, not empathy. "Got it." "Tell me more about that." "When did that happen?" Save the empathy for after the diagnosis is clear. Once the advisor understands what happened and what it means, *then* they can connect emotionally about it.

If the empathy comes too early, two things go wrong: the diagnosis gets short-circuited because the advisor stops asking questions when the emotional moment hits, and the prospect senses that the empathy is reflexive rather than earned.

## Diagnose to Find the GAP

The diagnostic phase exists to expose the GAP between where the prospect is and where they want to be. This is the connection to the GAP Framework (see `gap-framework.md`).

The advisor's questions during diagnosis aren't random. They're aimed at three things:

1. **Where is the prospect right now?** Specific, not vague. "We have about $2M in retirement accounts" is specific. "We're doing okay" is vague. The advisor digs until the picture is specific.

2. **Where do they want to be?** Specific, with a timeline. "We want to retire at 60" is okay. "We want to retire at 60 with $150K of annual income that we're confident won't run out" is better. The advisor pushes for specificity.

3. **What's it costing them to stay where they are?** This is where most advisors stop too early. The cost of the gap isn't just financial. It's emotional. It's relationships. It's lost time. It's the slow erosion of confidence in their own decisions. The advisor surfaces this.

The diagnostic approach exposes the GAP. Once the GAP is named, the prospect's relationship to it changes. They were carrying it before; now they're seeing it. The seeing is what creates the readiness for treatment.

## What This Module Constrains

When a block is generating content about advisor-prospect interactions:

**Sales-coaching content gets flagged.** Generic sales advice that violates the Physician Model fails. "Build rapport in the first 5 minutes" violates clinical distance. "Pitch the prospect on your unique value proposition" violates diagnose-before-prescribe.

**Tactical content reflects the diagnostic approach.** Specific moves the advisor can make during a discovery call should be diagnostic moves: questions to ask, things to listen for, signals that indicate underlying issues, ways to surface what the prospect isn't volunteering.

**The voice maintains the clinical frame.** When discussing prospects, the language is clinical, not therapeutic. "What's the prospect's actual situation?" rather than "How is the prospect feeling about their situation?"

**Pre-positioned solutions get flagged.** Content that walks the advisor through "here's what to say to convince the prospect" violates the model. The model says: figure out what's true, then figure out what to do about it.

## Common Failure Patterns

The model defaults to traditional sales coaching content because that's what most advisor content is. When generating content with the Physician Model loaded, watch for these:

**Failure: Empathy-first framing.**
- Wrong: "Start by making the prospect feel heard. Reflect their concerns back to them. Show you understand their pain."
- Right: "Start by understanding what's actually going on. Ask diagnostic questions. Note what they don't say as much as what they do. Empathy comes later, after the picture is clear."

**Failure: Pre-formed solutions.**
- Wrong: "When a prospect mentions retirement concerns, position your retirement income planning service."
- Right: "When a prospect mentions retirement concerns, ask what specifically they're worried about. Different concerns need different responses. The retirement income service might be relevant. It might not be. Find out before deciding."

**Failure: Sales-funnel language.**
- Wrong: "Move the prospect through your sales funnel by..."
- Right: "Run a thorough diagnostic. If the fit is real, the next step becomes obvious to both of you. If it isn't, an honest 'this isn't right for us' beats a fake-fit close every time."

**Failure: Performance-based confidence.**
- Wrong: "Project confidence by maintaining strong eye contact and a firm posture..."
- Right: "Confidence comes from the diagnostic work. If you've actually figured out what's going on, the conversation feels different — to you and to them. Body language follows substance, not the reverse."

## How the Physician Model Sits With Other Frameworks

The Physician Model is the *approach* — how the advisor shows up to the conversation.

Trust Stacking is the *worldview* — what trust actually is and how it gets built across the relationship.

The GAP Framework is the *diagnostic tool* — what the advisor is trying to surface during the diagnostic phase.

The Three Levels of Torment is the *qualification tool* — how the advisor evaluates the severity of what they're surfacing.

Offers vs. Proposals is the *response tool* — what the advisor does with what they've diagnosed.

These five frameworks work together. The Physician Model establishes the disposition; the others operate within that disposition. Content that loads any of these without the Physician Model can drift into sales coaching. Content with the Physician Model loaded stays anchored to the diagnostic approach.

## Reference for Content Creation

When generating content with the Physician Model loaded, useful framings:

- "Diagnose before prescribing" — direct invocation of the model
- "What's actually going on here?" — the diagnostic question
- "Most advisors solve too fast" — the failure pattern named
- "Save the empathy for later. The professional response during diagnosis is 'got it'." — the clinical-distance principle
- "If you don't understand the situation, you can't help them" — the foundational claim

The voice that pairs with the Physician Model is the weekday voice tone — clinical, observational, tactical. The model isn't preached. It's applied.
