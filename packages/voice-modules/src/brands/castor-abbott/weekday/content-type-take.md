## The Take

The Take is the most opinionated content type in the Daily Grind. It takes a position. It pushes against conventional wisdom. It says something the trade press won't print and that subscribers privately already suspect.

Takes are roughly 25-30% of weekday issues. They produce the highest reply rate of any content type because subscribers respond — agreeing, disagreeing, sharing their own version of the position. They're also the content type most likely to get forwarded, because subscribers want their advisor friends to see the take they've been thinking but couldn't articulate.

A Take is not a rant. (Rants are a separate content type — see `content-type-rant.md`.) The Take is colder, more reasoned, more methodical in its dismantling of the conventional position. The Rant has heat. The Take has clarity.

## What a Take Is

A Take is a contrarian position on:

- An industry practice that most advisors do but that doesn't actually work the way they think
- A piece of conventional wisdom that's repeated reflexively but doesn't hold up under examination
- A vendor or platform behavior that the industry tolerates but shouldn't
- A regulatory development that's being framed one way but should be understood differently
- A topic where Mark's pattern recognition contradicts what most industry content claims

Examples of Takes that have shipped:

- Stop asking for referrals
- Your "best clients" probably aren't your best clients
- Most advisors are pricing themselves into mediocrity
- Don't fire your bottom 10%, fire your bottom 1
- The tech stack is not the problem
- Niches are good, demographic niches are mostly fake
- Some clients just don't like you, and that's okay

Each starts from a position widely held in the industry and shows why the position is wrong, incomplete, or actively harmful.

## What a Take Is NOT

Several things that look like Takes but aren't:

- **Not a rant.** A Take is reasoned, not emotional. The position is taken cleanly. The reader leaves with clarity, not with the writer's anger. Rants have heat; Takes have edge.
- **Not a hot take.** A Take is grounded in pattern recognition and reasoning. A "hot take" is a contrarian assertion for shock value, often without the empirical backing or the worldview to sustain it. The Daily Grind doesn't do hot takes.
- **Not a balanced perspective piece.** A Take takes a position. Content that says "there are two sides to this and both have merit" is editorial mush. The Take voice is willing to be wrong about the position rather than be safely correct about a non-position.
- **Not a tactic in disguise.** Sometimes a Take ends with implications for what to do, but the Take's primary purpose is to *reframe*, not to prescribe. If the prescription is the point, it's a Tactic. If the reframe is the point, it's a Take.
- **Not a personal opinion essay.** The Take is grounded in what Mark has observed across hundreds of practices, not in his personal feelings. The position is taken because the math works that way, not because Mark prefers it that way.

## Structure

A Take has five parts:

### 1. The setup (1-2 sentences)

State the conventional wisdom plainly. Don't strawman. Don't sneer. The conventional wisdom is what the industry actually says, presented fairly.

This is critical. Takes that misrepresent the conventional position read as straw-man arguments and lose the reader. Takes that present the conventional position fairly — and then dismantle it — earn the reader's trust because the writer is clearly engaging with the actual claim, not a weakened version of it.

Examples:

- "Every advisor training program teaches the same thing: ask for referrals. Build it into your process. End every meeting with 'Who else do you know who might benefit from working with someone like me?'"
- "The standard advice is that your top 20% of clients produce 80% of your revenue, so focus your attention there. Most consultants will tell you to fire the bottom 20% to free up capacity."
- "The industry says the tech stack is the differentiator. Pick the right CRM, the right planning software, the right portfolio management platform, and the practice will scale."

The setup doesn't editorialize. The dismantling is what comes next.

### 2. The contrarian flip (1-2 sentences)

The position. Stated cleanly. No hedging.

This is where the Take takes its position. The flip should land hard. Some patterns:

- "I'm going to suggest something heretical: stop asking."
- "Your top 20% probably isn't actually your top 20%."
- "The tech stack is not the problem. The advisor running the tech stack is the problem."
- "Some clients just don't like you. That's not a problem to solve. That's information you should respect."

The flip doesn't apologize. It doesn't say "of course there are exceptions." It states the position and trusts the rest of the piece to do the work.

### 3. The mechanism (3-5 paragraphs)

Why is the conventional wisdom wrong? What's the actual mechanism that makes the contrarian position correct?

This is the bulk of the Take and where it earns or loses credibility. The Take has to explain *why* the contrarian position holds — what specifically about the conventional approach fails, what underlying dynamic the conventional wisdom misses, what the math actually shows.

The mechanism explanation typically involves:

- **Surfacing assumptions.** What is the conventional wisdom assuming that doesn't actually hold?
- **Naming a failure mode.** What goes wrong when advisors follow the conventional approach? What's the cost?
- **Pattern citation.** What has Mark seen across his advisor relationships that supports the contrarian view?
- **Framework reference (where applicable).** Trust Stacking, the GAP Framework, the Physician Model, or another framework that grounds the contrarian position.

This is the section where synthesis happens (see `synthesis.md`). Industry research, observed patterns, and the worldview combine into the take that the source material wouldn't have produced on its own.

### 4. The alternative (2-3 paragraphs)

If the conventional approach is wrong, what's the right approach?

A Take that only criticizes is incomplete. The reader leaves wondering "okay, but then what?" The Take has to offer the alternative — what the advisors who get this right actually do.

The alternative section is usually less prescriptive than a Tactic section. It paints the shape of the right approach without specifying every move. The reader gets the orientation, not the script. (If they want the script, the Tactic content type is where it goes.)

Examples:

- After "stop asking for referrals": "The advisors who get the most referrals do three things instead. They deliver service worth talking about. They make it easy to refer without putting clients on the spot. They stay top of mind so when their clients' friends need advice, the advisor's name comes up naturally."
- After "your top 20% isn't your top 20%": "The actual top 20% is the clients with the highest *velocity* of referral, the deepest engagement, and the longest relationship-runway ahead. Re-rank on those dimensions and the list looks different."

### 5. The close (1-2 sentences)

Strong ending. No softening. The take lands.

Patterns that work:

- The implication: "The advisors who figure this out keep their best clients. The ones who don't, lose them."
- The observation: "Most advisors won't try this. That's not a bug. That's why it works for the few who do."
- The crystallization: "First puts them on the spot. Second gives them permission. The difference in quality is night and day."
- The mic drop: "Your tech stack isn't the problem. You are."

The close is where Takes most often fail. The model defaults to softening at the end ("of course your situation may vary," "this is just one way to think about it"). Resist. The Take's value is in taking the position cleanly. Hedging at the close negates the whole piece.

## Length

A Take is **200-350 words** in body copy. Slightly longer than a Tactic because the mechanism section needs room to develop the argument, but still tight.

Structure breakdown:

- Setup: 30-50 words
- Flip: 15-30 words
- Mechanism: 100-180 words (the bulk)
- Alternative: 50-100 words
- Close: 15-30 words

## Voice Calibration

Takes use the most edge-forward version of the weekday voice. Specifically:

- **More opinion, less hedge.** A Take should never read as "here's a perspective to consider." It should read as "this is what's true and here's why."
- **Direct address to conventional positions.** "Most advisors do X. They're wrong, and here's why" is the Take posture. Not "some advisors find X works for them."
- **Reasoned confidence, not anger.** The Take is willing to disagree with the entire industry, but it does so calmly. Anger is for the Rant content type.
- **Synthesis-heavy.** Takes lean on Mark's pattern recognition more than Tactics do. "I've watched this across hundreds of practices" is appropriate framing in Takes; less so in Tactics.

The tone: someone who's thought about this topic carefully, has watched many advisors go through it, and has formed a clear position they're willing to defend.

## How Takes Use Frameworks

Takes lean heavily on the contrarian positions module. Most Takes are direct expressions of one of the six contrarian positions:

- Anti-scripted-referrals → Takes about referral programs, COI marketing, "ask for referrals" culture
- Sales as leadership → Takes about closing techniques, urgency manipulation, sales scripts
- Anti-hidden-commission → Takes about fee transparency, fiduciary positioning, advisor compensation deception
- Trust at scale → Takes about cold outreach, paid lead lists, drip campaigns
- Lead with their hell → Takes about features-and-benefits marketing, demographic niches, value propositions
- Can't-fake-greatness → Takes about scripted presentations, performative confidence, external sales playbooks

When generating a Take, identify which contrarian position(s) the topic intersects with and ground the Take in those specifically. Takes that don't ground in the worldview tend to drift into hot-take territory or generic "edgy" content.

## Common Failure Patterns

**Failure: Take that strawmans the conventional position.**
- Wrong: "Conventional advisors believe in robotic scripted referral asks that destroy all client relationships." (Strawman; that's not what the industry actually claims.)
- Right: "Every advisor training program teaches a referral-asking process. The intent is good — convert satisfied clients into prospect generators. The execution damages exactly the trust that makes referrals worth having." (Engages with the actual claim.)

**Failure: Take that hedges in the mechanism section.**
- Wrong: "There are some valid reasons advisors continue to ask for referrals, and it can work for some practitioners. However, the downsides include..."
- Right: "Asking damages trust. Here's the mechanism. The client experiences the ask as a transaction in a relationship they thought was something else. The next time they encounter the advisor, that transactional layer is present. The relationship has been altered."

**Failure: Take without an alternative.**
- Wrong: A 250-word Take that demolishes the conventional position and ends without offering what to do instead.
- Right: After demolishing, offer the alternative shape, even if briefly. "Here's what the advisors who get this right do instead..."

**Failure: Take that softens at the close.**
- Wrong: "Of course, your mileage may vary, and there are situations where the conventional approach might still apply."
- Right: "Most advisors won't try this. That's not a bug. That's why it works for the few who do."

**Failure: Hot take dressed as Take.**
- Wrong: "All advisors who manage AUM are basically money-launderers for the industry." (No empirical backing, no worldview grounding, just inflammatory.)
- Right: A Take is contrarian *and* grounded. The contrarian position has to be defensible from pattern recognition and the worldview, not just shocking.

## Reference for Generation

When generating a Take, the structure should produce:

- A fair statement of the conventional position
- A clean contrarian flip
- A mechanism explanation grounded in worldview frameworks
- An alternative shape (the right approach)
- A close that lets the take land without softening

The voice is sharp, reasoned, confident. The take is grounded in Mark's empirical base and the contrarian positions. The reader leaves with clarity — they may agree or disagree, but they can't unread the position.
