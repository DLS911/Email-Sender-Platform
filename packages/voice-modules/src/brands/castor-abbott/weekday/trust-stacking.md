## Trust Stacking

Trust Stacking is Mark's proprietary methodology for how a financial advisor builds genuine authority. It's the spine of any content that touches client acquisition, prospecting, or relationship-building. Tactics that violate Trust Stacking principles fail review regardless of how well they perform conventionally.

The eight principles below aren't a checklist. They're a worldview about what trust actually is and how it gets built. Most of the industry has the worldview wrong, which is why most of the industry's trust-building advice fails.

## The Eight Principles

### 1. Be trustworthy before being liked.

Don't try to be the prospect's friend first. Be credible.

The conventional sales playbook says rapport first, business second. Build the relationship, then introduce the transaction. The Trust Stacking position is that this is backwards.

A prospect who likes you but doesn't trust you is going to decide based on someone else they trust more. A prospect who trusts you but isn't sure they like you yet will hire you and grow into liking you over time. Trust is the foundation; affinity is the second floor.

The practical implication: your discovery calls shouldn't be friendly. They should be useful. The advisor demonstrates competence and judgment in the first 15 minutes. The prospect leaves the call thinking "this person knows what they're doing." Liking comes later.

### 2. Be competent before offering help.

Prove you understand the problem before you prescribe a solution.

Most advisor sales follow the pattern: prospect describes their situation, advisor immediately positions the firm's services as the answer. This signals two things: that the advisor wasn't really listening, and that the advisor has one solution for every problem.

The Trust Stacking move: the advisor demonstrates that they understand the prospect's specific situation in detail before any positioning happens. Not just the surface facts, but the underlying dynamics. The prospect leaves thinking "this person actually got what I was saying" — which is rare enough to be a competitive advantage.

### 3. Be a professional when prospects hide behind mistakes.

Prospects often present a sanitized version of their situation. They downplay how much they've left undone. They blame an old advisor or a complicated family situation. They protect their ego.

The amateur advisor lets this slide and works with the surface story. The professional advisor — kindly but directly — finds the actual problem and asks the uncomfortable questions.

This isn't aggressive. It's the difference between a doctor who accepts "I'm pretty healthy" at face value and a doctor who runs the actual labs. The prospect doesn't want a yes-man. They want someone who will see clearly enough to actually help.

### 4. Bring people to a clear decision point.

Make sure they know exactly what they're deciding.

Most advisor sales meander. The prospect leaves the call without knowing what comes next, what's expected of them, or what they're agreeing to. The advisor leaves equally unclear. Both spend the next two weeks in awkward follow-up.

The Trust Stacking move: at the end of the conversation, the advisor names the decision the prospect needs to make and the timeline for making it. Not pressure tactics. Just clarity. "Here's what we'd do together. Here's the cost. Here's how the next step works. Take a week to think it over and let me know."

This respects the prospect. Vagueness disrespects them.

### 5. Sales is leadership, not "selling".

You're guiding, not convincing.

The pejorative version of sales is "convincing someone to buy something they don't need." Trust Stacking rejects this entirely. If the prospect doesn't need what you're offering, the answer is no, and a professional says no.

If they do need what you're offering, your job is to lead them to that recognition clearly. They'll often resist — not because the answer is wrong, but because making decisions about money and life is hard. The advisor's job is to lead them through the resistance to the decision they actually need to make.

This is leadership. Convincing is manipulation. The two look superficially similar but feel completely different to the prospect, and prospects can tell which one is happening.

### 6. You can't fake greatness.

Everyone knows instantly when they're with a professional.

There's no script that makes a mediocre advisor sound like a great one for more than a few minutes. The prospect picks up on the difference within the first call. Tone of voice. Quality of questions. Comfort with silence. Willingness to disagree. Speed of pattern recognition. These can't be faked.

The implication for content and tactics: stop trying to find the magic words or the perfect framework. The work that produces greatness is internal. The external behavior is a reflection of the internal state.

This is uncomfortable for industry content because the industry sells external solutions to an internal problem. Trust Stacking won't pretend otherwise.

### 7. Trust is purchased.

Not in dollars. In hours of inner work that lets you stop performing and start being present.

Most advisors are performing during prospect meetings. They're saying what they think they're supposed to say, asking what they think they're supposed to ask, presenting what they think they're supposed to present. The prospect feels the performance. It registers as not-quite-trustworthy.

The advisors who do this well have stopped performing. They're actually present. They're listening. They're noticing things. They're willing to be quiet. They're willing to disagree. The prospect feels the presence. It registers as trustworthy.

The path from performing to present is inner work. There's no shortcut. The hours go into self-awareness, into ego management, into the practice of being comfortable in uncertainty. Most advisors don't do this work. The ones who do are the ones who close consistently.

### 8. Care more about what they want.

More than you want a "sale."

When the prospect senses that the advisor wants the transaction more than the prospect's outcome, trust collapses. When they sense the opposite — that the advisor genuinely cares about what the prospect wants more than about closing — trust amplifies.

This can't be performed. Prospects can tell.

The practical implication: an advisor who's willing to recommend that the prospect *not* hire them when it's clearly not a fit will close more deals overall than one who tries to close every deal in front of them. The non-closing recommendations build credibility that pays off across years.

## How Trust Stacking Shapes Content

When generating content for a block with Trust Stacking loaded, several specific implications hold:

**Tactics that violate principles get flagged.** A prospecting tactic that involves manipulating prospects, scripting away authenticity, or treating sales as adversarial fails Trust Stacking. The editor block flags this and asks for revision.

**Generic "build rapport" advice doesn't pass.** Building rapport without first establishing competence reverses principle 1. Content that recommends "open with small talk to make them comfortable" gets flagged.

**Pressure tactics fail.** Urgency manipulation, fear-of-missing-out, scarcity scripts — these all violate principles 5 and 8. The audience can smell them anyway, but Trust Stacking makes the violation explicit.

**Inner work gets named.** Most industry content treats sales as external behavior. Trust Stacking surfaces the internal component (principle 7) when relevant. This produces content the audience finds rare and valuable.

**Content can disagree with industry conventional wisdom.** Trust Stacking provides the framework for why "everybody is doing X" is not a good reason to do X. Content can argue against widespread practices when they violate Trust Stacking principles.

## What Trust Stacking Is Not

A few clarifications on scope:

- **Not a sales methodology in the procedural sense.** Trust Stacking doesn't tell you what to say in minute four of the discovery call. It's the principles that any specific tactical approach must respect. Specific scripts and procedures live in the tactic content; Trust Stacking governs them.
- **Not anti-sales.** Trust Stacking is *for* selling — for closing the right deals with the right prospects in a way that produces durable client relationships. It's against the manipulative-sales subset of the broader sales discipline.
- **Not aspirational/inspirational.** Trust Stacking is operational. The principles describe what works, why it works, and what doesn't work. Read it as a craft document, not a motivational document.
- **Not a checklist for review.** Don't grade content against the eight principles in a literal way. Use Trust Stacking as the lens through which the content gets evaluated. Some pieces will reference one or two principles directly; most will reflect the worldview without naming it.

## Reference for Content Creation

When generating content with Trust Stacking loaded, useful framings:

- "The advisors who do this well..." rather than "you should..."
- "I've watched this work across hundreds of practices..." for empirical grounding
- "Conventional wisdom says X, but the prospects you're after are looking for Y..." for reframes
- "This isn't a script you can perform. It's something that becomes true when..." for the internal-work principles
- Direct contradictions of common industry practices when warranted ("Skip the scripted referral ask. The advisors winning at this don't have one.")

The voice that pairs with Trust Stacking is the weekday voice tone — sharp, clinical, observational. The eight principles aren't preached. They're applied as the lens.
