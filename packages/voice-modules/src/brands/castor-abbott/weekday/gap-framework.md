## The GAP Framework

The GAP Framework is the diagnostic tool that powers the Physician Model. It answers three questions the advisor must surface during prospect conversations and bring to clarity in their own thinking before any positioning happens:

1. **Where is the prospect now?**
2. **Where do they want to be?**
3. **What is it costing them to stay where they are?**

The GAP is the distance between question 1 and question 2. The cost is question 3. The advisor's job is to expose the GAP — not to solve it. Solving comes later. The exposure of the GAP is what creates the prospect's readiness to engage seriously.

## Why This Framework Exists

Most advisors lose deals because they try to solve before exposing. A prospect describes their situation in vague terms; the advisor immediately starts positioning services that might address what was described. The prospect, sensing the rush, retreats. The deal goes cold.

The GAP Framework is the discipline that prevents this. The advisor stays with the diagnostic phase until the gap is clearly exposed — to the prospect, not just to the advisor. Once the prospect can see the gap clearly, they engage with the conversation about closing it. Until they can see it, they're going to resist anything the advisor offers.

The framework is small. The discipline is hard. Most advisors know the framework intellectually and still fail to apply it because applying it requires staying in discomfort longer than feels natural.

## Question 1: Where Are They Now?

The current state. Specific, not vague.

The advisor's job is to push past the prospect's narrated version of their situation to the actual situation. Most prospects don't lie; they generalize. The generalization is where the diagnostic work breaks down.

Examples of generalization vs. specificity:

- Generalization: "We've got a decent amount saved up."
- Specificity: "Combined, we have $1.4M across retirement accounts and $300K in a brokerage account. There's also $250K equity in our home. No other major assets."

- Generalization: "Our portfolio is doing okay."
- Specificity: "We're 80/20 stocks/bonds. Most of the equity is in three index funds at Schwab. The bond position is in a target-date fund I haven't really looked at in three years. We've never rebalanced."

- Generalization: "We work with a guy who handles things."
- Specificity: "We've got an advisor at Edward Jones we've worked with since 2003. He calls maybe twice a year. The fee is 1% on the equity portion, which I think but don't know for sure. We've never had a written plan."

The questions that produce specificity:

- "What does that look like specifically?"
- "What are the actual numbers?"
- "When did that last get reviewed?"
- "What's the breakdown?"
- "Tell me more about that piece."
- "What does 'doing okay' mean — what's been the return, roughly?"

The advisor pushes for specificity without making the prospect feel grilled. The frame is curiosity and competence: "I can't help if I don't understand the actual picture. Help me understand the actual picture."

## Question 2: Where Do They Want to Be?

The desired state. Also specific, also with a timeline.

Most prospects haven't thought through this question carefully. They have vague aspirations: "comfortable retirement," "financial security," "leave something to the kids." The advisor's job is to take these vague aspirations and pull them into specifics.

Examples of vague aspirations vs. specific outcomes:

- Vague: "We want to retire comfortably."
- Specific: "We want to stop working at 62. We want to spend $120K a year, in today's dollars, until we're 90, with high confidence we won't run out. We want to keep the lake house and not have to sell it."

- Vague: "We want to leave something to the kids."
- Specific: "We want each of the three kids to inherit at least $500K, in today's dollars, after taxes. We don't want them to inherit until they're at least 35. We want the inheritance to be invested, not spent immediately."

- Vague: "We want to be financially secure."
- Specific: "We want to be in a position where one of us could lose our job tomorrow and we'd still be on track for our other goals without panic."

The questions that produce specificity:

- "Specifically, what does that look like for you?"
- "When do you want that?"
- "How much income do you need?"
- "What would 'enough' actually mean?"
- "If everything went perfectly for the next ten years, what would your life look like?"
- "What are you most afraid of losing?"

The same disciplined push for specificity, applied to the desired state. Without specificity, there's no GAP. There's just vibes.

## Question 3: What Is It Costing Them?

This is where most advisors stop too early. They surface the current state and the desired state, identify the gap, and start positioning. The Physician Model says: not yet. There's a third question that has to land before positioning works.

What is it actually costing the prospect to stay where they are?

The cost has multiple dimensions:

**Financial cost.** What's the difference, in actual dollars, between staying on the current path and getting to the desired state? This is usually the easiest to surface. "If you keep doing what you're doing, you'll have $X. If you do what we're talking about, you'd have $Y. The difference is $Z, which means..."

**Time cost.** What does the gap mean in terms of time? "If you stay on this path, you'll hit your retirement goal at 65. If you make these changes, you could hit it at 62. That's three years of life that look very different."

**Emotional cost.** What's the prospect carrying because of the gap? Most advisors miss this entirely. The prospect is often carrying a low-grade anxiety they've gotten used to: a gnawing sense that something's not quite right, an unspoken worry about what happens if a market downturn hits at the wrong time, a guilty feeling that they should have figured this out earlier. Naming this is uncomfortable. It's also where the deepest leverage is.

**Relationship cost.** Often the gap is creating tension in the prospect's relationships. Their spouse is anxious. Their adult children are worried. Their business partner is asking questions. The gap isn't just personal; it's relational.

**Confidence cost.** The gap erodes the prospect's confidence in their own decision-making. Every quarter that passes without addressing it reinforces the sense that they don't know what they're doing. This compounds.

The questions that surface cost:

- "What does it feel like to have this on your mind?"
- "What conversations have you been having with your spouse about this?"
- "What's your worst-case scenario you're worried about?"
- "How long has this been bothering you?"
- "What would change in your life if this were handled?"

The cost question is the hardest part of the GAP Framework to do well. It's also the most consequential. Until the prospect feels the cost — not just intellectually but emotionally — they won't make the change.

## When the GAP Becomes Visible

When the three questions land, the prospect sees something they didn't see before. They saw their situation as "doing okay." Now they see a specific gap, with a specific cost, that they haven't been engaging with.

This is the diagnostic moment. The prospect's relationship to the conversation changes. They go from "let me hear what you might offer" to "okay, what do we do about this?"

The advisor doesn't manufacture this moment. They surface what's already true. The gap was always there. The prospect just hadn't seen it clearly. The framework is the way to clarity.

Once the moment lands, the advisor has earned the right to talk about treatment. Not before.

## What This Framework Constrains

When generating content with the GAP Framework loaded:

**Discovery call content centers on diagnostic questioning.** Specific questions the advisor can ask to surface each of the three GAP questions. Not generic "rapport building" advice.

**Tactical advice respects the framework's sequence.** Content that recommends positioning services before exposing the GAP fails. Content that recommends "show ROI" before establishing the cost dimension fails.

**The cost question gets full treatment.** Most industry content stops at "show the gap." The GAP Framework's contribution is the cost dimension. Content that loads the GAP Framework but treats the cost dimension as secondary fails to use the framework correctly.

**The voice maintains the diagnostic frame.** When describing the framework or applying it, the voice is clinical and observational, not motivational. "Here's what to ask" not "here's how to inspire."

## Common Failure Patterns

When generating content with the GAP Framework loaded, watch for these:

**Failure: Treating the framework as a sales tool.**
- Wrong: "Use the GAP Framework to build urgency in the prospect's mind so they're motivated to close."
- Right: "Use the GAP Framework to surface what's actually true. The motivation that follows is the prospect's, not something you manufactured."

**Failure: Skipping the cost question.**
- Wrong: "Once you've identified the gap between current state and desired state, present your services."
- Right: "Once you've identified the gap, the diagnostic isn't done. You haven't yet surfaced what staying in the gap is costing them. That's the question that has to land before positioning works."

**Failure: Treating cost as purely financial.**
- Wrong: "Calculate the dollar cost of inaction and present it as the value of working with you."
- Right: "Cost has financial, time, emotional, relationship, and confidence dimensions. The financial dimension is often the easiest to surface but rarely the one that drives the decision. The emotional and relationship costs are usually what tips a prospect from 'considering' to 'committing'."

**Failure: Pre-formed cost narratives.**
- Wrong: "Lead the prospect to recognize the cost of inaction by walking them through this script..."
- Right: "Ask questions that let the prospect surface their own cost. The cost is theirs, not yours. Your job is to make the questions that let them see what they've been carrying."

## How the GAP Framework Sits With Other Frameworks

The GAP Framework is the diagnostic structure. The Physician Model is the disposition that allows it to be used well. Trust Stacking is the worldview that determines what to do with what's surfaced. The Three Levels of Torment is the qualification framework for evaluating the severity of the gap.

Loaded together, these produce a coherent approach to prospect interaction. Loaded without each other, the individual frameworks can be misapplied as sales tools. The combined loading is what produces the Castor Abbott voice on this topic.
