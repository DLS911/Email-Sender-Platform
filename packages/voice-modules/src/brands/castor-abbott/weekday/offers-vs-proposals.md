## Offers vs. Proposals

There's a critical distinction in how an advisor goes to market that most advisors miss. Mark calls it Offers vs. Proposals.

A **proposal** asks the market to be interested. It says: "Here's what I do. Here's why it might matter. Please consider whether you'd like to engage with me about this." The advisor is the one initiating; the prospect is the one being asked to find this relevant.

An **offer** is a response. It says: "You're carrying this specific pain. Here's how I help with it." The advisor isn't initiating; the prospect's existing pain is initiating, and the advisor is responding to it.

Most advisor marketing is proposals dressed as offers. The advisor genuinely thinks they're offering something — but they're actually proposing, because they're trying to convince a market to be interested in services rather than responding to pain that already exists.

The reframe: don't try to convince a market to be interested. Find out what they're already buying, and sell them what they're already buying. Find pain that's already Recently, Frequently, and Intensely experienced, and offer relief from that specific pain.

## The Difference in Practice

Consider two ways an advisor might go to market:

**Proposal version:**

> "I'm a CFP serving high-net-worth families. I provide comprehensive financial planning, investment management, and tax-efficient strategies tailored to your unique situation. Let me know if you'd like to schedule a complimentary consultation."

This is a proposal. It's asking the market to find this interesting. It assumes that "comprehensive financial planning" is a thing people are actively looking for. It treats the advisor's services as the starting point.

**Offer version:**

> "If you've sold a business in the last 18 months and are dealing with a sudden seven-figure problem you didn't have before — what to do with the money, how to think about the tax mess, how to talk to family about it — that's specifically what we work on."

This is an offer. It's responding to a pain that exists. The pain is Recently active (last 18 months), likely Frequently surfacing (people don't think about $7M for a few minutes a week), and Intensely felt (sudden wealth is genuinely disorienting). The advisor isn't proposing; they're answering an unanswered question someone is already asking.

The first will struggle to fill a calendar. The second produces a steady flow of qualified inbound. The difference isn't tactics. It's the underlying disposition: am I trying to make you interested, or am I responding to what you're already feeling?

## Why Most Advisor Marketing Is Proposals

Most advisor marketing is proposals because the advisor designed it from the inside out. They thought about what they offer, then asked how to position it. The frame is: "I have services; how do I make people want them?"

The Offers reframe inverts: "What pain is already active? What problem is already costing people sleep? What question is already in their head?" Once those are identified, the question of services becomes secondary. Services are the response to the pain, not the starting point.

The frame matters because it changes everything downstream. A proposal-frame advisor is constantly trying to educate the market on why they should care. An offer-frame advisor is responding to people who already care because their pain is real.

## What Counts as an Offer

For something to be an offer (not a proposal), it has to meet specific criteria:

**It addresses pain that's Recently active, Frequently experienced, and Intensely felt.** (See the Three Levels of Torment.) If the pain isn't qualified on these dimensions, the "offer" is actually a proposal — you're asking the market to find a low-grade concern interesting enough to act on.

**It speaks to a specific situation, not a demographic.** "Successful executives" is a demographic. "Executives whose company just got acquired and now have $5M in suddenly-vested stock with a 60-day window to do something about it" is a specific situation. The first is a proposal frame. The second is an offer.

**It uses the prospect's language for the pain, not the advisor's language for the solution.** "Comprehensive financial planning" is the advisor's language. "Figuring out what to do with the proceeds from selling my business" is the prospect's language. Offers use the prospect's language.

**It positions a response, not a service.** "Here's how we help with that specific situation" is a response. "Here's our service offering" is a service. The response frame is the offer frame.

## The Niche Implication

The Offers vs. Proposals framework has a direct implication for niche selection. Most advisors approach niches as a marketing differentiation tactic: pick a vertical, brand around it, claim expertise.

The Offers reframe is different: pick a niche based on where you can identify Recently/Frequently/Intensely active pain that you can answer. The niche isn't the marketing positioning; it's the pain that's already there and underserved.

This is why "demographic niches" (serving doctors, serving tech executives, serving women) often fail to produce the differentiation the advisor expected. The demographic by itself doesn't carry pain. A doctor who's not facing any specific acute issue isn't going to find "I serve doctors" particularly compelling.

What works is **transition niches** — moments when life changes and acute pain shows up. Liquidity events. Inheritance. Divorce. Sudden retirement. Business sales. A new diagnosis. These are moments when the pain is Recent, Frequent, and Intense by definition. The niche is the moment, not the demographic.

A practice positioned around "advisors for tech executives" is a proposal. A practice positioned around "advisors for tech executives whose company just IPO'd or is about to" is an offer.

## The Discovery Call Implication

Offers vs. Proposals also reframes how the advisor approaches the discovery call.

The proposal-frame advisor enters the call thinking: "I need to convince this prospect that my services are worth engaging with."

The offer-frame advisor enters the call thinking: "I need to find out if this prospect's pain matches what I help with. If yes, here's how I respond. If no, here's an honest acknowledgment that we're not the right fit, and possibly a referral."

The second produces a fundamentally different conversation. The advisor is calmer. The prospect feels less pitched. The honesty about fit (or lack of fit) builds trust whether or not the deal closes.

## What This Framework Constrains

When generating content with Offers vs. Proposals loaded:

**Marketing/positioning content reflects the offer frame.** Content recommending demographic niches, broad service positioning, or "convince the market" tactics gets flagged. Content focused on identifying pain that's already active, then designing responses to it, is on-frame.

**Content avoids hustle-marketing language.** "Stand out from the crowd," "differentiate yourself," "make yourself unforgettable" — these are proposal-frame language. They assume the work is convincing. The offer frame says: don't convince. Respond.

**Niche content reflects the transition framing.** When discussing niches, the framework should surface that demographic niches without specific transition-pain underneath them tend to fail. Transition-based niches tend to work because they have qualified pain built in.

**Content treats the prospect as someone with existing pain, not as someone who needs to be convinced.** The voice across content reflects this: respect the prospect's autonomy, respect their pain as real, respond to it rather than try to manufacture a different response.

## Common Failure Patterns

**Failure: Treating Offers as a marketing tactic rather than a frame.**
- Wrong: "Use the Offers framework to write better marketing copy that converts."
- Right: "The Offers framework isn't marketing copy. It's a fundamental shift in how you think about going to market. The copy follows from the shift, but if you adopt only the copy without the shift, the proposal frame leaks back in within months."

**Failure: Calling proposals offers.**
- Wrong: "I offer comprehensive financial planning to high-net-worth families." (This is a proposal phrased as an offer.)
- Right: "If you're dealing with [specific Recently/Frequently/Intensely active pain], here's how we work on that."

**Failure: Assuming any niche is an offer.**
- Wrong: "Pick a niche to make yourself more competitive."
- Right: "Pick a niche based on where you can identify Recent/Frequent/Intense pain you can answer. The niche by itself isn't the value; the response to active pain is."

**Failure: Marketing content that tries to "create demand."**
- Wrong: "Build awareness in your target market about why they need a financial advisor."
- Right: "Find prospects whose existing pain you can answer. The need is already there. Your job is to be findable when they're already looking."

## How Offers vs. Proposals Sits With Other Frameworks

This framework is the strategic-positioning layer. The GAP Framework is the diagnostic layer that operates inside a discovery call. The Three Torments are the qualification layer for evaluating a specific prospect's pain. The Physician Model is the disposition. Trust Stacking is the worldview.

Offers vs. Proposals operates higher up — at the level of "how does the advisor go to market in the first place?" The frameworks compose: an advisor with an offer-frame practice runs Physician-Model discovery calls using the GAP Framework and the Three Torments to qualify. The whole thing is coherent.

Without the offer frame, the rest of the frameworks can be applied tactically to the wrong prospects. With the offer frame, the prospects who arrive in the door are already qualified at the strategic level, which makes the diagnostic work more productive.
