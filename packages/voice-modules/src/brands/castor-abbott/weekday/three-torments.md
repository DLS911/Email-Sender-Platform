## Three Levels of Torment

Not all prospect pain is equal. A prospect who mentions a financial concern in passing is in a different situation than a prospect who's been losing sleep over the same concern for two years. The advisor needs to distinguish between them, because the conversations should run very differently and the resources spent on each should differ.

The Three Levels of Torment are the qualification framework: **Recency, Frequency, Intensity.** Each dimension surfaces something different. Together they produce a clear picture of whether this is a mole problem or a cancer problem.

## The Three Dimensions

### Recency: When did this start?

The first question is when the pain became active.

A prospect who's been worried about retirement for 15 years has a different relationship to that worry than one whose 401(k) balance just dropped 20% last quarter and is now reconsidering everything. The recency tells the advisor about the prospect's window of action.

Recency questions:

- "When did this start bothering you?"
- "When did you first notice this was an issue?"
- "What changed recently that brought this to the surface?"
- "Has this been on your mind for a while or is it new?"

What recency reveals:

- **Long-recency pain (years).** The prospect has been carrying this for a long time. They've probably failed to act on it before. The advisor needs to understand why action hasn't happened — and what's different now if action is happening.
- **Recent recency (months).** Something changed. A market move, a life event, a conversation, a tax bill. Surface what changed. The change is often the actual trigger for the conversation.
- **Imminent recency (weeks or days).** Something acute is happening. Often the prospect is closer to a decision than they appear, because they're already feeling the urgency themselves. The advisor doesn't need to manufacture urgency; it's already there.

### Frequency: How often does this come up?

The second question is how often the pain enters the prospect's awareness.

Some pains are intermittent — the prospect thinks about them once a quarter when statements arrive, then forgets. Other pains are constant — the prospect carries them daily. The frequency tells the advisor about the prospect's psychological burden and their motivation level.

Frequency questions:

- "How often does this come up for you?"
- "Do you find yourself thinking about this regularly, or does it come up periodically?"
- "How often have you and your spouse talked about this?"
- "When you think about your finances, how often does this specific concern surface?"

What frequency reveals:

- **Low frequency (monthly or less).** The prospect isn't actively burdened. They have an issue but they're not motivated to act yet. The advisor should be honest about this. Trying to manufacture urgency on a low-frequency pain produces a soft close that falls apart later.
- **Medium frequency (weekly).** The pain is active but not overwhelming. The prospect is aware but functioning. This is the most common state for serious prospects. The motivation is real but the timeline is flexible.
- **High frequency (daily or near-daily).** The pain is dominant. The prospect is carrying it constantly. This is often the state of a prospect who's ready to make a major change soon. The advisor's job is to confirm this is real and to be ready to move at the prospect's pace.

### Intensity: How much does it cost them?

The third question is how severe the pain is when it does come up.

Some pains are background hum — present but tolerable. Other pains are debilitating — the prospect can't function around them. The intensity tells the advisor about the urgency and the size of the change the prospect is willing to make.

Intensity questions:

- "When this comes up, how much does it bother you?"
- "What's the worst version of this you've experienced?"
- "Has this caused conflict in your relationships or affected other parts of your life?"
- "What does this really cost you?"
- "Are you sleeping okay?"
- "What does your spouse think about this?"

What intensity reveals:

- **Low intensity.** The prospect is uncomfortable but coping. The change they're willing to make is incremental. Don't push for major changes here.
- **Medium intensity.** The prospect is genuinely bothered. They're motivated to address this seriously. Standard engagement.
- **High intensity.** The pain is interfering with the prospect's life — sleep, relationships, work, decision-making. Major change is on the table. The advisor should be ready for fast-moving conversations and significant decisions.

## The Mole vs. Cancer Distinction

The Three Levels of Torment together produce a qualification:

**Mole problem:** Long-recency, low-frequency, low-intensity pain. Annoying but tolerable. The prospect isn't going to make major changes for this. The advisor's response should match: light engagement, periodic check-ins, no urgency.

**Cancer problem:** Recent-or-imminent recency, high frequency, high intensity. Acute, dominant, costly. The prospect is going to make major changes soon, with or without this advisor. The advisor's response should match: serious engagement, prepared resources, willingness to move at the prospect's pace.

Most prospects fall in between. The framework isn't about labeling prospects mole or cancer; it's about understanding where on the spectrum they are and matching the engagement to the reality.

## Why This Framework Matters

Most advisors qualify prospects on financial dimensions: AUM threshold, income, asset complexity. These matter, but they're insufficient. A $5M prospect with mole-level pain is going to take 18 months to close (if at all) and produce a low-engagement client. A $1M prospect with cancer-level pain is going to close in three weeks and become an evangelist.

The Three Torments are the dimensions most advisors miss. The financial qualification is the first filter; the torment qualification is what actually determines whether this is a serious prospect right now.

The framework also protects the advisor's calendar. An advisor who treats every $5M prospect as equally serious will burn out trying to convert mole-level prospects into cancer-level decisions. The advisor who can quickly distinguish the two can invest deep effort in the prospects who are actually ready and light effort in the prospects who aren't yet.

## What This Framework Constrains

When generating content with the Three Levels of Torment loaded:

**Qualification content reflects the three dimensions.** Not just financial qualification. Not just "are they a fit?" The qualification has to surface the recency, frequency, and intensity of their pain.

**Tactical content treats prospects differently based on torment level.** Mole-level prospects get a different conversation than cancer-level prospects. Content that treats every prospect the same fails to use the framework.

**Urgency manipulation gets flagged.** The framework explicitly says: don't manufacture urgency. If the pain is mole-level, the urgency isn't there. Trying to create it produces a fake close. Content that recommends urgency creation tactics violates the framework.

**The voice respects the diagnostic frame.** When describing the framework or applying it, the voice is observational and clinical. "Here's what to listen for" not "here's how to push them harder."

## Common Failure Patterns

**Failure: Using the framework to manufacture urgency.**
- Wrong: "Once you've identified that the prospect's pain is high-recency and high-intensity, leverage that urgency to close the deal quickly."
- Right: "Once you've identified that the prospect's pain is high-recency and high-intensity, match their urgency. They're already moving fast. Be ready to move with them. If you discover the pain is actually low-frequency despite being high-intensity, slow down — they're not as ready as the intensity suggested."

**Failure: Treating the three dimensions as a sales scoring tool.**
- Wrong: "Score the prospect 1-5 on each of recency, frequency, intensity. Multiply the scores. Above 60 is a hot lead."
- Right: "The dimensions don't multiply into a score. They produce a picture. A high-intensity, low-frequency prospect is different from a medium-intensity, high-frequency prospect, and they need different conversations."

**Failure: Stopping at the first dimension.**
- Wrong: "Ask the prospect when their concerns started. The recency tells you what to do next."
- Right: "Recency alone is insufficient. A long-recency, high-intensity pain is a different prospect than a long-recency, low-intensity pain. Surface all three before drawing conclusions."

**Failure: Pretending to know the dimensions without asking.**
- Wrong: "Most $5M prospects who reach out are dealing with cancer-level pain. Treat them accordingly."
- Right: "Don't assume. The questions that surface recency, frequency, and intensity take ten minutes and produce a picture you can't get from the financial profile alone."

## How the Three Torments Sit With Other Frameworks

The Three Levels of Torment qualify the pain that the GAP Framework surfaces. The Physician Model is the disposition that allows the advisor to ask the questions productively. Trust Stacking is the worldview that determines what to do with the qualified prospect.

A prospect with cancer-level torment that the advisor can genuinely help: serious engagement, treatment plan, real conversation about working together.

A prospect with mole-level torment: honest acknowledgment that this isn't the right time for major change, possibly periodic check-ins, no pressure.

A prospect with cancer-level torment that the advisor can't help: honest referral to someone who can. Trust Stacking principle 8 — care more about what they want than about closing.

The frameworks compose. Loaded together, they produce a coherent diagnostic and qualification approach. Loaded individually, they can be misapplied.

When generating content with the Three Levels of Torment loaded, useful framings:

- "Recency, Frequency, Intensity" — the three-dimension summary
